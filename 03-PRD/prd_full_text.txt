1. Executive Summary
Nyay-Sathi is designed as an access, intelligence and coordination layer around India's existing justice and legal-aid
ecosystem. It should not replace ICJS, e-Prisons, e-Courts, NALSA/SLSA/DLSA processes, police systems, prisons
or courts. The platform's distinctive value is the combination of family-facing access, voice/vernacular support,
statute-grounded legal information, potential eligibility screening, human legal review and routing into authorized
legal-aid support.
(cid:127) Family access through web/mobile and voice-oriented channels.
(cid:127) Case intake and understandable case-status/timeline information.
(cid:127) Document upload and structured extraction with user/PLV verification.
(cid:127) Versioned legal-source registry and citation-grounded RAG.
(cid:127) Rule-based eligibility screening first; ML only after controlled evaluation.
(cid:127) Legal-aid/lawyer/PLV routing with workload, language, district and SLA considerations.
(cid:127) Human-override logging, auditability and bias/fairness evaluation.
(cid:127) Prison-side confirmation channel and URC-ready reporting.
(cid:127) Rights education, surety/bail-bond navigation and post-release support hooks.
2. Problem Statement
(cid:127) Families may struggle to understand case status, legal terminology, documents, deadlines and available legal-aid
pathways.
(cid:127) Existing justice infrastructure is fragmented from the perspective of a family member who needs a simple,
vernacular, actionable interface.
(cid:127) Legal information may exist but not be translated into plain-language next steps.
(cid:127) Potential statutory-review signals can be missed when no one notices or raises the relevant issue.
(cid:127) Voice/feature-phone access and low-literacy support are central accessibility requirements for the intended user
journey.

(cid:127) If no approved source supports the answer, the system must abstain and route to human support.
RAG rule: Retrieval is a prerequisite for legal explanation; free-form model knowledge must not be treated as

13. Eligibility / Screening Engine
The first production-oriented implementation should be a versioned, rule-based screening engine. ML should be
a later, separately evaluated layer.
(cid:127) Normalize case facts.
(cid:127) Load applicable rule versions.
(cid:127) Evaluate conditions and exceptions.
(cid:127) Return a potential signal, not a court outcome.
(cid:127) Show detected facts, rule/source, missing information and confidence band.
(cid:127) Require human verification for consequential actions.
(cid:127) Keep legal rules separate from UI code.
14. Confidence & Explainability
(cid:127) Use High/Medium/Low or equivalent bands for screening confidence.
(cid:127) Define confidence as information/model reliability, not probability of a court granting bail.
(cid:127) Show why a signal was raised.
(cid:127) Show which facts are missing or unverified.
(cid:127) Show exact legal source and version.
(cid:127) Record reviewer agreement/disagreement.
15. Human-in-the-Loop
AI signal fi legal reviewer/PLV/lawyer fi agree or override fi reason fi audit log fi evaluation dataset.
(cid:127) Every override requires a reason.
(cid:127) Never hide AI-human disagreement.
(cid:127) Use disagreements for audit and model/rule improvement only under approved governance.
(cid:127) AI must remain advisory.
16. Bias & AI Evaluation
(cid:127) Build a held-out evaluation set before trusting an ML layer.
(cid:127) Measure retrieval accuracy, citation correctness, hallucination rate, rule accuracy and human agreement.
(cid:127) Track false positives and false negatives.
(cid:127) Audit performance across operational dimensions such as district, offence category, language and legal-aid
availability where appropriate.
(cid:127) Have legal/ethics reviewers inspect disagreements before scale.

36. Final Product Principle
NYAY-SATHI should be built as a trustworthy civic-tech system where AI detects, verified law explains,
humans verify, and the justice system decides. The project's credibility comes from interoperability,
legal-source governance, human control, accessibility, security and measurable operational improvement—not
from claiming that an AI can predict or decide court outcomes.
